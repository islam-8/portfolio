Abstract
This monograph details the research, firmware architectures, and hardware-software co-design of a software-defined, low-latency, full-duplex walkie-talkie platform (VoxLink) built on the 32-bit Xtensa LX106 microcontroller (ESP8266). Wireless voice transmission in low-cost distributed environments often suffers from major packet timing jitter, router bottlenecks, and high bandwidth consumption. We propose a serverless, peer-to-peer network utilizing raw connectionless UDP broadcasts on a localized 802.11 mesh. PCM voice signals are acquired via direct I2S DMA to bypass CPU polling. On-device compression is performed using a 4:1 IMA ADPCM quantization encoder, dropping the data requirement from 128kbps to 32kbps. We evaluate the mathematical step-size adaptation, design a 40ms jitter buffer, and present real-time packet loss simulations.

I. Audio Signal Acquisition & Nyquist-Shannon Sampling

The fundamental foundation of digital speech transmission begins with the digitisation of analog acoustic waves. According to the Nyquist-Shannon sampling theorem, to prevent aliasing, the sampling frequency $f_s$ must be greater than twice the highest frequency component of the analog signal ($f_{max}$):

f_s > 2 \cdot f_{\max}

For human speech, the essential intelligibility bandwidth is contained below $4\text{ kHz}$. We select an industry-standard sampling rate of $f_s = 8\text{ kHz}$ with 16-bit PCM resolution, yielding a raw uncompressed serial stream bandwidth of:

\text{Bandwidth} = 8000 \text{ Hz} \cdot 16 \text{ bits/sample} = 128\text{ kbps}

To maintain real-time execution without timing jitter, the Xtensa LX106 CPU cannot spend cycles polling the ADC. We configure the INMP441 MEMS microphone to stream directly to the MCU's internal **I2S (Inter-IC Sound) controller**. The controller utilizes **Direct Memory Access (DMA)**, transferring raw audio samples directly into SRAM ring buffers in the background. Once a 20ms block (exactly 160 samples) is filled, an interrupt fires to hand the buffer over to the DSP compressor.

II. IMA ADPCM 4:1 Compression Calculus

Uncompressed audio streaming imposes heavy overhead on wireless nodes. To prevent mesh channel congestion, we implement the **Interactive Multimedia Association Adaptive Differential Pulse Code Modulation (IMA ADPCM)** algorithm.

Rather than transmitting absolute 16-bit values, ADPCM transmits the difference (residual) $d(n)$ between the current sample $x(n)$ and a predicted value $\hat{x}(n)$. This difference is quantized into a 4-bit nibble using a variable quantization step-size $ss(n)$.

d(n) = x(n) - \hat{x}(n) \quad \rightarrow \quad c(n) = \text{quantize}\left(\frac{d(n)}{ss(n)}\right)

The step-size $ss(n)$ adapts after each sample using a lookup table containing 89 step coefficients (ranging from 7 to 32767). If the predicted error is high, the step size increases exponentially to track high-frequency transits. If the error is small, the step size collapses to maximize resolution in quiet periods. This adaptive approach avoids the quantization clipping associated with static delta modulation.

By converting each 16-bit sample into a 4-bit nibble, VoxLink achieves a flat **4:1 compression ratio**, bringing the required voice stream bandwidth down from **128kbps to exactly 32kbps**, easily fitting inside the localized 802.11 transport budget.

V. Mesh Protocol Optimization Study

Standard TCP/IP sockets demand continuous handshake agreements ($SYN \rightarrow SYN-ACK \rightarrow ACK$). In highly mobile walkie-talkie meshes, where devices move past obstacles, drop-outs are common. Enforcing TCP retries causes severe latency cascades: a single dropped voice packet stops the entire queue, raising latency past $500\text{ms}$.

VoxLink resolves this by using **connectionless UDP broadcasts**. Packets are thrown to the network's broadcast domain. If a packet is lost, the receiver does not wait. Instead, the Jitter Buffer triggers a **Packet Loss Concealment (PLC)** routine, repeating the last successfully received block with a $3\text{dB}$ attenuation factor, keeping speech flowing smoothly:
